## Title: A recommendation for chiirin (Chiikawa calligraphy practice)
## Date: 2026-08-21
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

I haven't gone in all that deep, but I'm having fun with it. Day after day, all I ever think about is "AI and Chiikawa." It's not that I've become some fanatic, but I keep at it. The day before yesterday, I started what I call "chiirin" (Chiikawa transcription practice). You know how everyone assumes that "Chiikawa" is just one of those "small and cute" things that seem to be everywhere?

Well, I thought so too, but I was completely wrong. For starters, if someone asked you, "Mom, try drawing one," could you actually draw one of those seemingly commonplace "Chiikawa" pictures? Sure, you could draw something similar, but you probably wouldn't get it right. What you'd end up with is something that bears no resemblance at all, something distinctly "un-Chiikawa-cute."

In other words, the way the creator, Nagano-sensei, draws "Chiikawa" is incredibly difficult. It's the mark of a true master, a work of consummate skill. I had a hunch this might be the case, so I tried copying one while looking at the original, and honestly, I just couldn't do it. It never came out looking good.

So I hit upon the idea of doing what the boss of my household does with calligraphy, which is to practice by transcribing a masterwork of the brush. For those "Chiikawa" pictures to exist on the page, there had to have been an "action," a specific movement of the brush, that gave rise to each of those lines. To transcribe a piece of calligraphy is to study the finished work closely and then reproduce, in my own hand, the "action" that once brought it into being. Copying a piece of writing is really about reproducing the act itself.

While turning over these knotty thoughts in my mind, I sit and draw the characters from "Chiikawa." The hardest parts are the outlines and the eyes. I can see exactly what the eyes are supposed to look like, but I just can't reproduce them. The position, the size, the thickness of the line, the tilt of each circular eye, all of it is the handiwork of a master. But then again, to learn is to imitate.

I believe that if I keep up my transcription practice every day, like copying out a sutra, I'll surely arrive at a state of "Chiikawa mastery, effortless and free." I really do recommend it, this daily "chiirin" (Chiikawa transcription practice).
