## Title: Not precious merely because it is deep.
## Date: 2026-09-01
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

This is really just a passing thought, so I'd like you to read it as nothing more than an "image."

People love to talk about things being deep or shallow. And whenever they do, the general assumption is that deep is good. It's not quite that shallow is bad, but shallow carries this whiff of an easy, unimpressive shortcut, as if it just hasn't managed to reach the depths yet. That seems to be the idea.

Long ago, I traveled around to various places with an agriculture teacher. From the northern countries down to the south and west, we even made it all the way to Europe. Among those stops was, I think, the gardens of the Palace of Versailles, or maybe it was Marie Antoinette's vegetable patch. They grow the vegetables eaten by people of the very highest nobility, they said. How on earth did we get permission to visit a place like that? Without really understanding, I just followed the teacher, and it turned out he simply ignored the walls and gates and walked right in.

All sorts of crops were being cultivated there, but the teacher declared every last one of them a failure. "See, they're growing these to make the roots go deep. But the truth is, you want the roots to spread out shallow and wide and soft."

Wanting to nurture something carefully and deeply because it's precious, he said, is a scholar's way of thinking. Making roots go deep is pointless, and it doesn't make the vegetables taste any better. What you want is to grow them so the fine, fluffy root hairs spread out. This too was surely a theory grounded in practice, so there may well be other schools of thought, but to me it was moving. Ever since then, I've come to think about deep and shallow from a different angle than everyone else.

I mean, whether you're doing the crawl above the Mariana Trench, said to be the deepest place in the world at over ten thousand meters, or paddling in a pool two meters deep, it's the same swimming, isn't it?
