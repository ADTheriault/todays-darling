## Title: It wasn't Jomon pottery, but still
## Date: 2026-11-25
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

Besides the workbook and the picture diary, our summer vacation homework included something called the "free project." Naturally, I was the kind of kid who waited until the very last days of August, then panicked ("Oh no, what am I going to do?!") and could barely manage to scramble through filling in the blanks of the workbook. And yet, for some reason, I was brimming over with a desire to make my free project into something fun. Every year at summer's end I'd produce some peculiar creation, and I think it may have gone over well with about three of my classmates. But a "work" short on time, effort, skill, and passion was never going to win anyone's applause.

Among those rather sorry memories, the one I remember most vividly is the "Jomon pottery." People in ancient times would knead clay, roll it out thin like a snake, coil it round and round, and shape it into a vessel. Maybe pressing in the cord patterns is what makes it Jomon, but a schoolkid in the Showa era had no room for that sort of thing. No time, no skill. Just make something more or less bowl-shaped out of dirt. Then, if you stuck it in a bonfire, you'd surely end up with "pottery." If shaggy-bearded Jomon folk could do it way back when, how hard could it be? I had more than enough foolishness to think exactly that.

I'm a little amazed no grown-up scolded me, but I gathered up scraps of wood, set them alight, and built a bonfire. Right in the middle, I placed "the thing that would soon become pottery." No matter how many branches I added, no matter how long it burned, "the thing that would become pottery" only gave off billows of steam, grew hot, and slowly dried out. A hot, dry clod of earth... and that's all it ever stayed, no matter how long I waited.

The person I am now could understand what was missing and why, and might even manage a slightly more respectable failure. But the elementary schooler I was back then couldn't do any more than that, that day. What I had was a lump of dirt, charred black in places, that might, if you squinted, pass for a lukewarm pot. Even to me, its maker, it looked nothing like Jomon pottery, however you viewed it, and I never brought it to school as my summer project. Still, the person I am now would like to tell that boy: you know, it was good that you did it, just as it was.
