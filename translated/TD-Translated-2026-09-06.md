## Title: Walking while weaving through the crowd.
## Date: 2026-09-06
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

When you head out to somewhere like Shibuya on the weekend, the crowds are just enormous. I'd say maybe sixty percent of them are travelers from overseas. They probably walk at a different pace, spreading out in a different way than city people here in Japan do. To keep from bumping into anyone, you have to make a few adjustments of your own.

But even with the young people who look properly Japanese, you'll get several of them walking abreast in a row, and that too is something you have to deal with. Slipping through the gaps becomes your job. This is the sort of thing we've probably always been doing, unconsciously, in the middle of crowds. An ordinary level of congestion I'm used to, but when it gets truly packed, you end up dodging this way and that like a rugby match, and it's quite tiring. If you were only walking, you'd just be burning the energy of the exercise, but once you add in the swerving and the shifts in speed, your body ends up working all sorts of subtle little muscles, and I imagine your nerves are hard at work too.

(These were the thoughts that occurred to me as I walked up Dogenzaka, and once I started walking with the deliberate intent of writing about it for Hobonichi, I realized just what a tiring thing I was doing.)

People who live out in the regions say "the city wears you out," but wouldn't the busy downtown districts in the regions be exactly the same? In places where the distance between one person and the next grows close, you use your nerves on "relationships" without even noticing, and that must be what tires people out.

So yes, I really do think that, well, I won't go so far as to say deep in the mountains, but a space that isn't crowded, what Kazuto Ataka calls "sparse space," is probably something human beings need, like a bath or like nourishment. Hobonichi may be living its life within the city, but lately we've become very conscious of cultivating that kind of "sparse space" ourselves.
