## Title: Language has always been squiggly.
## Date: 2026-09-28
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

"Chiikawa" is short for "chiisakute kawaii yatsu," a small and cute little thing. "Mirishira" is short for saying you didn't know even one millimeter's worth about something. And "tamageta," meaning to be astonished, apparently comes from the idea of your very soul vanishing.

Oh, I see, is that so, I find myself thinking. Being startled really is like having your soul slip away, isn't it. Looking into the origins of words is quite a lot of fun. A famous example is the word for twilight, "tasogare." It comes from an old phrase meaning "who is that person over there?", the hour when it grows dark and you can no longer tell one face from another. There's something rather lovely about that one too, don't you think?

"Arigatou," thank you, comes from "arigatashi," which is to say that something is difficult to have or to come by. You're grateful because someone has done for you a thing that rarely happens. These are the sorts of things I've been taught, or overheard, over the years.

But what I actually want to talk about today lies one step further back than all of this.

"Tasogare" is made up of two words, "who" and "that person." I understand that much, I can follow it that far, that's all perfectly clear. But then, where did the words for "who" and "that person" themselves come from? Where did "ari" and "katashi" originate? Around when did they first appear, and how did they take shape?

I'm aware that this kind of research exists, of course. But when you start tracing it back the way you'd follow the branches of an evolutionary tree in biology, you inevitably arrive at "well, what was there in the very beginning?", drifting toward something like the origin of life itself.

And the thing is, words didn't evolve according to some precise set of laws. They've been used and bent and misspoken and forgotten, repeating that kind of change over and over like living creatures, until they became what they are now. That's just how it works, and I find it fascinating.

A dictionary tells you what a "dog" is, but that entry is really just a provisional casebook, a record of "this is the kind of thing everyone has agreed to call a dog." Isn't there something delightful in all this bewildering messiness?
