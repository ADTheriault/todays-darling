## Title: Again: the story of when I came in 8th place.
## Date: 2026-09-30
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

I've told this same story over and over again. It's about the days when I was completely absorbed in fishing.

There was a local bass fishing tournament held at Lake Kawaguchi. It was a regular competition, held a few times a year, and I think around 150 people usually took part. Me, a proper middle-aged man, would line up at the reception desk from around six in the morning, waiting for the start alongside younger folks and students who were all my seniors when it came to fishing. I can't remember whether the tournament ended at one in the afternoon or some other time, but the idea was that you'd have your catch weighed in turn and compete for a ranking.

As a newcomer, I was one of the older working adults, but I was one of the worse ones. I usually placed around 80th, squarely in the "thanks for participating" group. But then, one day, I had a feeling I'd actually caught a decent amount, and when I had it all weighed in and the results came out, I'd landed in 8th place.

This whole rambling piece I've written could really just come down to those two characters: "8th place."

Normally, watching sports and the like, I only ever paid attention to the battle for first. I'd feel crushed when someone lost in 2nd, or disappointed by a 5th. So when someone like me came in not first, not fifth, but 8th, I was so genuinely happy that tears actually welled up.

Eighth place is amazing, I realized. When you're not a competitor yourself, just watching the fight for the top, 8th place is something you can't even begin to think about. But once you become the one actually taking part, you understand just how happy 8th place can make you (and I suppose you'd also understand the frustration of 8th, too).

When you look at Mount Fuji, it's normal to look at the summit. But Fuji exists together with those vast, sweeping foothills too. I looked it up, and even the fifth station, where you can go by bus, is about 2,300 meters above sea level. If you were to walk all the way to the top, even the fifth station is quite something.

"Understanding that there are things you can only imagine because you're not the one involved, and then, on top of that, becoming able to imagine what it's like for the person who is." Maybe that's the kind of practice we're all doing. And yet, honestly, we still don't really understand it at all.
