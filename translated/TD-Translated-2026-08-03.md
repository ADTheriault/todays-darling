## Title: Sweet potato tempura with a firm batter.
## Date: 2026-08-03
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

When people praise tempura, they always say things like, "The batter is light and crisp." Crunchy, no hint of oiliness, light. That's what everyone reaches for. And I agree. I really do.

That said, the tempura you get at udon chains and places like that isn't light at all, and it certainly isn't crisp. If anything, the batter is thick enough to have opinions of its own, and rather than light, you'd have to call it hard.

But is that bad? Is it unpleasant? Not to me. I actually want this kind of tempura too. Especially the sweet potato ones. I'm fine with batter so hard it clunks against your teeth. And I'm certainly not going to talk about how it "doesn't taste of oil." I'll even go and pile on extra tempura crumbs.

They're both tempura, and they're both good, aren't they? No need to rush to declare that this one is proper and that one isn't. They're both delicious.

With just about anything, there's a kind of "accepted standard for what counts as good." Things that have been around a long time naturally develop one. Tempura, sushi, grilled fish, soba, udon, all of them come with a set of "words of praise" that everyone more or less knows.

But even if you know those words, I think it might be better to set them aside for a while. "Springy, chewy udon" is delicious, of course, but there's no reason we can't also discover the deliciousness of "soft, sloppy udon."

Being unhygienic or unclean is another matter, but I really do think that finding "deliciousness" from a different angle deserves to be valued more.

Come to think of it, the things people call "old-fashioned" include "firm pudding," "omelettes that aren't all soft and runny," and "salmon that's too salty," don't they? I'm sure there are plenty more if you go looking.

Right now, at two in the morning, I want a bowl of udon with hard tempura on top.

Thank you for coming to Hobonichi again today. Though honestly, the ordinary words of praise turn out to be right more often than not.
