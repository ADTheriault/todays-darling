## Title: More on the karaoke approach to calligraphy copying
## Date: 2026-09-11
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

I wrote before that karaoke might be a kind of "copying from a master's calligraphy," and honestly, it really is. When you're not just listening but tracing the song with your own voice, singing it, things you never noticed before start to catch your attention.

The same thing happened with "Melody" by Koji Tamaki, a song I love, and so do my karaoke friends. It opens like this: "In this town where you were, the one I loved so much, that song I still love so much, I can still hear it now." And right there, a little uncertainty creeps in.

What was it that was "loved so much," the "you," or "this town"? Then it goes on, "that song I still love so much, I can still hear it now," and this second "love," I think, is probably about "that song." Two "loves," and a "you," a "town," and a "song." You can sing it happily enough without thinking too hard about what modifies what, but the moment you start interrogating it, you lose the thread.

I feel the same way about Mariya Takeuchi's "Eki." There's a line, "that I was the only one who loved," and I'm never quite sure what emotion I'm supposed to sing it with. Two years after the breakup, she happens to catch sight of her old lover. Did she realize "I was the only one who loved him, one-sidedly"? Or did she realize "back then, he loved only me"? The line can be read either way.

With both "Melody" and "Eki," I imagine that for the person who wrote them, it's something they could explain without a second thought: "Oh, it means this." But I think the students doing their "calligraphy copying" through karaoke start to want to sing, in their own voices, whatever it was that was being sung in the "model text."

For what it's worth, as the apprentice loitering at the temple gate, doing my own "copying," I leave both songs "unresolved." Because I think that vagueness is part of what makes a song good.
