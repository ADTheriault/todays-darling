## Title: Will humans win against it?
## Date: 2026-09-07
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

A car runs far faster than any human ever could. It can also travel far further than a person on foot. But nobody, as far as I know, ever went out of their way to marvel that "the automobile has surpassed the speed of man." Nor is there anyone out there training their legs, refusing to be outrun by a car.

The electric washing machine, too, labors away far more patiently than a human, and it gets the clothes far cleaner than someone scrubbing away on a washboard. Yet nobody ever raised a fuss that humanity had at last been overtaken by a machine in the art of washing.

Come to think of it, when I try to remember, the desktop calculator went on sale and nobody said, "Look, this thing calculates faster than a human!" Nobody said, "Maybe it's completely left the abacus in the dust." Of course it was fast. Fast was fine. That was simply the point.

The car, the washing machine, the airplane, the calculator, weren't they all things that humans poured their hearts into developing precisely because they wanted to "lose" to them?

By the time the computer, the "electronic calculator," had advanced far enough that human victory in a game of chess was starting to look shaky, people were, I think, even looking forward to the day when a human would finally lose.

Before long, computers really did begin to "beat" humans, at chess and at shogi alike. And it was around then that people started saying, "Wait, this feels a little different," and talk of wariness toward machines grew more and more common.

Now that today's "generative AI" has spread everywhere, it's being discussed all over the place as an "unprecedented problem" for humankind. There seems to be a great deal being said, and a great deal that ought to be said. But I've decided to start from the premise that we are meant to lose. After all, these are things we built precisely so that we could "lose, and be better off for it."
