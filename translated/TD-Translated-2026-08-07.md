## Title: I spent years overlooking "Chiikawa."
## Date: 2026-08-07
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

There's a phrase, "swing at the good ones," which Mister himself, Shigeo Nagashima, once brushed onto a signature board. Hit the good pitches without fail, leave the bad ones alone, and good results will follow. "Chiikawa," for instance, was one of those good pitches.

Right now I'm working my way not only through every volume of "Chiikawa" but also through the manga the author drew before it, and I find myself impressed at every turn. Lately what's been quietly moving me is the way she handles "pauses" and "lingering resonance." The artist who draws it is wonderful, and I think the reader who gets to savor it is lucky too. This is something that always existed in Japanese four-panel and eight-panel manga, so it isn't as though it's some brand-new technique, but this "expression through what isn't drawn," akin to haiku or tanka, truly lands in the reader's heart with remarkable skill. If Ms. Itsuki Natsui, the haiku poet, were to read "Chiikawa," I bet she could discuss it as poetry.

But you know, here I am saying that "Chiikawa" and "AI" are all I think about these days, and yet until a few months ago this was a pitch I let sail right past me, dismissing it with a "yeah, I know it, so what?" Just a called strike. "Chiikawa" means "small and somehow cute," and because that almost seems to say it all, you just breeze past with "nice, kind of cute" and leave it at that.

Then one day I learned why the character "Momonga" plays up the cutesy act the way it does, and suddenly I was hooked, thinking, "wait, what is that all about?!" Come to think of it, Hobonichi's relationship with "Chiikawa" goes all the way back to the "Gentle Towel" in 2022, yet even then I was only looking at it thinking, "oh, that's nice, cute." My curiosity was fast asleep as I gazed at "Chiikawa." I hadn't taken my stance with any intention of swinging the bat (lesson learned).

◆Now, a question, no. 003: "What's your favorite way to play?" Think about it freely in your own way and jot it down in your notebook. And if you'd like, write it in an email and send it my way.

Thank you again today for coming to Hobonichi. Though there's a limit to how many pitches you can hit on personal curiosity alone.
