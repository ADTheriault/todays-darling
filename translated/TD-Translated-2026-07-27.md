## Title: The mythological world of "Chiikawa"?
## Date: 2026-07-27
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

I'd been dying to see the "Chiikawa" movie, counting down the days. On a Sunday afternoon, at a theater full of parents and kids, I'd booked myself a seat as a lone old man cashing in his senior discount.

People often point out that "Chiikawa" isn't just some cute little world for children, all "small and adorable." It's actually a notch more tangled than that, a work with real venom in it. And yet the young fans adore it, and it's true that you can lose yourself in it and simply have fun, forgetting your troubles for a while. The way it holds innocence and complexity together at once, that's what makes it so impressive.

So then, what do the children who came all the way to the theater to watch "Chiikawa" feel, sitting still in their seats for ninety-nine minutes, before they head home? I was curious about that too.

When the film ended and the house lights came up, everyone, adults and children alike, was quiet. Not a bit of chatter. Someone who'd experienced this before me described it as "like a wake." I felt that way too, but of course, it was a good time, I swear!

What if we thought of "Chiikawa," I found myself wondering, as a "world of myth" being told here in the twenty-first century? What if you took the ordinary things scattered around everyday life, the small and cute, the frightening, the strange stories you've heard somewhere before, and wove them all together into a "mythic world," what would that look like?

Books from the library, snacks and prepared foods from the convenience store, all the tools and fixtures of schools and parks, the odd behaviors of grown-ups, the songs that drifted through your memories. Taking materials like these, just as the ancients built "Greek myth" and "Indian myth" and the "Kojiki," isn't "Chiikawa" giving birth to a "Japanese myth" for our own age? And doing it, no less, in the language of everyday spoken speech?

Maybe this piece I'm writing today makes no sense to you at all, maybe you can't grasp what on earth I'm going on about. And I think that's perfectly fine, because, you see, "Chiikawa" is poetry and it is myth, and so it's fine.

Thank you, as always, for coming to Hobonichi today. Oh, and the truly important thing about "Chiikawa" is, above all else, the food!
