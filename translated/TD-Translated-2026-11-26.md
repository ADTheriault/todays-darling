## Title: Swim where the water is
## Date: 2026-11-26
## Author: Shigesato Itoi
## Content:

There's an old Japanese saying about "swimming lessons on the tatami." The point is that if you want to swim, you can study the theory on the floor mats all you like, but you'll never actually learn to swim that way.

So if you want to learn to swim, what do you do? That struck me as a surprisingly interesting question. If you want to take up chess, you read a beginner's guide. If you'd like to try knitting, you start with a book. These days, "look it up online" is just as common. Right. I get the feeling we've become a little too used to "Read first!" whenever we want to do anything. Even when you're starving and dying for a big pot of chanko stew, you start searching on your phone before you've bought a single ingredient, don't you? Maybe "Read first!" is just a habit we picked up from learning everything out of textbooks.

But here's what I love about it: "I want to be able to swim" is one thing "Read first!" can't make happen. Just to test it, I went ahead and asked an AI. And I have to hand it to it, it didn't tell me to read first.

"Get used to putting your face in the water. Then practice breathing out underwater. Then relax your body and learn what floating feels like. Then put together your leg movements, arm movements, and breathing. Practice in a safe, shallow pool with an instructor."

Still, reading this alone leaves you short on just about everything. You have to go somewhere there's water, and better yet, find yourself a good teacher. Ask an AI or don't, it's not something you can take care of inside your room. Manuals, textbooks, teaching methods, tips and tricks: go looking and you'll find no end of them, and they keep getting better. But floating on the water, swimming through it, seems like something you can only learn by practicing with the water itself. That said, if you think you'll do better having read up on it first, well, I suppose there's no harm in trying that too.
